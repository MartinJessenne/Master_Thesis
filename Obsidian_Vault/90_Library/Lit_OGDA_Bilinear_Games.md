Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent in Zero-Sum and General-Sum Bilinear Games

Étienne de Montbrun, Jérôme Renault
November 24, 2022

Abstract
We study the convergence of Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent in unconstrained bilinear games. In a first part, we consider the zero-sum case and extend previous results by Daskalakis et al. [5], Liang and Stokes [15] and others: we prove, for any payoff matrix, the exponential convergence of OGDA to a saddle point and also provide a new, optimal, geometric ratio for the convergence. We also characterize the step sizes inducing convergence, and are able to deduce the optimal step size for the speed of convergence. In a second part, we introduce OGDA for general-sum bilinear games: we show that in an interesting class of games, either OGDA converges exponentially fast to a Nash equilibrium, or the payoffs for both players converge exponentially fast to +ˆž (which might be interpreted as endogenous emergence of coordination, or cooperation, among players). We also give sufficient conditions for convergence of OGDA to a Nash equilibrium. These conditions are used to increase the speed of convergence of a min-max problem involving a matrix A, by introducing a general-sum game using the Moore-Penrose inverse matrix of A. This shows for the first time, at our knowledge, that general-sum games can be used to optimally improve algorithms designed for min-max problems. We finally illustrate our results on simple examples of Generative Adversarial Networks.

1 Introduction
Min-max optimization is receiving a lot of attention, due in particular to the popularity of generative adversarial networks (GANs, introduced in [11]) and adversarial training (see for instance [16]). In the original version of GANs, two neural networks are in competition: a generator that aims at generating data as close as possible to some real data, and a discriminator whose goal is to differentiate between generated and true data. Because the discriminator wants to maximize a loss that the generator wants to minimize, GANs can be seen as a min-max, or zero-sum game, problem.

Formally, consider a "payoff" function $g:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ and assume we want to find $y^{*}$ achieving $\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}g(x,y)$. Assuming the zero-sum game associated to g has a Nash equilibrium (i.e., a saddle-point), the game-theoretic approach is to find an equilibrium, i.e. $(x^{*},y^{*})$ in $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$ such that:
$\forall x, \forall y, g(x, y^{*}) \le g(x^{*}, y^{*}) \le g(x^{*}, y).$ (1)

A natural algorithm to search for such an equilibrium is the Gradient Descent-Ascent algorithm (GDA): given a small parameter $\eta>0$ and a starting point $(x_{0},y_{0})$, define inductively the sequence:
$\forall t\ge0,\begin{cases}x_{t+1}&=&x_{t}&+&\eta\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}(x_{t},y_{t}),\\ y_{t+1}&=&y_{t}&-&\eta\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(x_{t},y_{t}).\end{cases}$

Most GANs currently use (stochastic) Gradient Descent Ascent under the hood. In some cases, GDA was acknowledged to converge in average to the solution of the min-max problem in several zero-sum games [18]. However, it is well known that even for bilinear functions $g(x,y)=x^{T}Ay$ with $\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{n}$, GDA may exhibit a cyclic behavior and the last iterate may not converge ([7] for example). A nice variant of the GDA is the Optimistic Gradient Descent-Ascent algorithm (OGDA):
$\forall t \ge 0, \begin{cases} x_{t+1} &=& x_{t} + 2\eta \nabla_{x}g(x_{t}, y_{t}) - \eta \nabla_{x}g(x_{t-1}, y_{t-1}), \\ y_{t+1} &=& y_{t} - 2\eta \nabla_{y}g(x_{t}, y_{t}) + \eta \nabla_{y}g(x_{t-1}, y_{t-1}). \end{cases}$ (2)

This optimistic variant has been introduced by Popov in [20] and rediscovered in the GAN literature ([5] for example). A challenge is then to determine when OGDA converges to an equilibrium of the game. In this paper, we focus on the bilinear unconstrained case, where $\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}$, $\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and payoffs are bilinear.

We first consider in section 2 the zero-sum case where $g(x,y)=x^{T}Ay$ for some matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$. The difficulty here is not to find a saddle-point of g (this is fairly easy: take $x^{*}=y^{*}=0)$ but to prove or disprove the convergence of OGDA to a saddle-point in this simple setup. In this context, Daskalakis et al. [5] proved the last iterate convergence of OGDA, in the sense that for each $\epsilon>0$ one can find $\eta>0$ and T large enough such that $(x_{T},y_{T})$ (as in (2)) is $\epsilon$-close to a Nash equilibrium. Liang and Stokes [15], as well as Mokthari et al. [17] proved that in the case of a square invertible matrix A, OGDA converges to (0,0) at exponential speed. Peng et al. [19] extended the analysis to non square matrices.

We extend in Theorem 2.4 of section 2.1 the previous results of the literature. For any matrix A and any initial conditions, OGDA does converge, and the limit is a Nash equilibrium that we characterize (and is independent of $\eta$). Moreover, the speed of convergence is exponential and we exhibit the exact geometric ratio for the convergence, as a function of the step size $\eta$ and the matrix A, improving the previous ratios obtained for square invertible matrices. We also characterize the range of step sizes where convergence holds, extending the previous values considered for $\eta$. We believe these results of last iterate convergence matter because they show a strong stability of the OGDA algorithm for any matrix: not only OGDA can be used to find $\epsilon$-equilibria, but they will not exhibit cyclic behaviors within the possibly large set of Nash equilibria.

In section 2.3, Theorem 2.4 is compared with the previous existing results, and in section 2.4 Theorem 2.5 extends the results of Theorem 2.4 to the bilinear case where $g(x,y)=x^{T}Ay+b^{T}x+c^{T}y+d.$ In section 2.5, we compute as a function of A, the step size $\eta$ optimizing the geometric ratio of convergence. Whereas the largest step size considered so far in the literature was $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ (with $\mu_{\max}$ the largest eigenvalue of $AA^{T}$), it turns out that the optimal step size is always at least $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$, and greater than $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ as soon as $AA^{T}$ has several nonzero eigenvalues. The optimal ratio varies between $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ to 1, and is characterized as a function of $\frac{\mu_{\min}}{\mu_{\max}}$ where $\mu_{\min}$ is the smallest positive eigenvalue of $AA^{T}$.

The proofs, in Appendix, are based on a precise spectral analysis of the linear system with variable $(x_{t+1},y_{t+1},x_{t},y_{t})$, one difficulty being to control the angles between complex eigenspaces in order to obtain proper rates of convergence.

We then consider OGDA for general-sum bilinear games. We stick here to the case where $\mathcal{X}$ and $\mathcal{Y}$ are Euclidean spaces, and in section 3 we introduce the natural extension of OGDA for bilinear general-sum games. It is easy to see that OGDA need not always converge to a Nash equilibrium here, and we introduce in Theorem 3.4 a very simple variant of OGDA, called DOGDA (Double Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent), which always converges to a Nash equilibrium in a bilinear general-sum game $(x^{T}Ay,x^{T}By)^{1}$. We then come back to OGDA and give in Theorems 3.8 and 3.11 sufficient conditions for convergence to a Nash equilibrium, when all eigenvalues of $B^{T}A$ are real negative.

In section 3.4, we come back to the problem of finding $y^{*}$ achieving $\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(x,y)$, where $g(x,y)=x^{T}Ay+b^{T}x+c^{T}y+d$ is bilinear. One can use the zero-sum results of section 2 and use OGDA for the zero-sum game with payoff functions g for player 1 and -g for player 2. We obtain the convergence of $(y_{t})_{t}$ to an optimal $y^{*}$ at exponential speed with ratio in $[\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 1)$ depending on $\frac{\mu_{\min}}{\mu_{\max}}$. But one can also consider OGDA for the general-sum game with payoff function $g_{1}=g$ for player 1 and $g_{2}(x,y)=-x^{T}(A^{\dagger})^{T}y$ where $A^{\dagger}$ is the generalized (Moore-Penrose) inverse of A. It turns out that Theorem 3.11 applies here, and for this OGDA as soon as $\eta\le1/2$ the sequence $(y_{t})$ converges to the same optimal $y^{*}$, with a geometric rate of $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ when $\eta\simeq1/2$. This shows the interest of introducing OGDA for general-sum games: the speed of convergence to a solution of a Min-Max problem can be improved if one considers a well suited general-sum game, instead of the natural associated zero-sum game.

The last part of section 3 considers OGDA for a particular class of payoff matrices, including in particular the case of common payoff games where $A=B$ (implying that there is a potential for coordination or cooperation among players), or more generally the cases where $B=\alpha A$ for some $\alpha\ne0.$ It is proved that for this class of games, either OGDA converges to a Nash equilibrum, or under OGDA the payoff for both players tends to $+\infty$ exponentially fast as $t\rightarrow+\infty$. We believe this result is a meaningful extension of the convergence of OGDA, since both players having $+\infty$ payoffs can be seen as a generalized Nash equilibrium of the game and a very desirable outcome of the interaction.

In section 4 we illustrate our results on stylised versions of Generative Adversarial Networks. We first recall the theoretical GANs and WGANS setups, and then illustrate our results on linear instances of Wasserstein GANs problem in the spirit of [5]. We show in particular on an example how introducing a general-sum game using $B=-(A^{\dagger})^{T}$ improves the speed of convergence of the OGDA. We also discuss limitations of the bilinear model with respect to real GANs applications.

We finally conclude and present a few possible research directions in section 5. The proofs are in the Appendix.

Other Related Works:
Besides [5], [15], [17] and [19], Zhang and Yu [23] also consider convergence of gradient methods in bilinear zero-sum games. Gidel et al. [10] study GANs through variational inequalities, with a convergence ratio not better than in [15]. In [21], Rakhlin and Sridharan introduced an algorithm called Optimistic Mirror Descent (OMD), based on the Mirror Descent, and showed that it is a no-regret algorithm. Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent (OGDA) is a particular case of OMD, for which the regularizer used is the 2-norm. Another algorithm close to OGDA is the Extra-Gradient method (EG) introduced by Korpelevich in [14]. Both OGDA and EG can be seen as approximations of the Proximal Point (PP) method. In [17], Mokhtari et al. showed again the exponential convergence of PP, OGDA, and of EG, for square invertible bilinear games, for a single particular value of $\eta$, and with an exponential bound of convergence greater than ours. Another article studying Extra-Gradient, including a stochastic setting, is the one of Hsieh et al. [12].

Beyond bilinear games, some papers consider OGDA for concave/convex games. In particular, Daskalakis et al. [7] study the stability of fixed points of the dynamics. Non-concave/non-convex setting exhibit problematic properties: [13] shows there may exist attractors of OGDA containing no stationary points, and [8] shows that finding an approximate local minmax equilibrium is PPAD-hard. However, in [9], Diakonikolas et al. showed positive results in a new class of non concave/non convex minmax problems. Many studies where also done in a compact settings, that is when $\mathcal{X}$ and $\mathcal{Y}$ are compact sets. In [6], Daskalakis et al. proved the convergence of an Optimistic version of Multiplicative Weight Updates on the simplex, for an appropriate value of the learning rate. Later, Wei et al. improved their results in [22], by showing the convergence for any learning rate smaller than some constant. Finally, in a recent paper [1], Anagnostides et al. consider the convergence of a projected version of OGDA for general-sum games, where after each iteration, a projection on the simplex is applied.

Notations 1.1. We use the following standard notations:
$\mathbb{R}_{+}$ denotes the set of non-negative reals and $\mathbb{R}_{-}$ the set of non-positive reals. $A^{T}$ denotes the transpose of a matrix A in $\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$. If A is square, $Sp(A)$ is the set of complex eigenvalues of A, and $\rho(A)=\max\{|\lambda|,\lambda\in Sp(A)\}$ is the spectral radius of A. Given column vectors $x, x^{\prime}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ or more generally in $\mathbb{C}^{n}$, we use the Hermitian scalar product $\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle=x^{T}\overline{x^{\prime}}$ where $\overline{x^{\prime}}$ is the complex conjugate of $x^{\prime}$ and the norm $\|x\|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}$. Given two matrices A and B of the same size, we denote by $\mathcal{S}(A,B)$ the set $Sp(B^{T}A)\cup Sp(AB^{T})$ and we simply write $\mathcal{S}(A)$ for $\mathcal{S}(A,A)$. $I_{n}$ denotes the identity matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$.

2 Zero-Sum Bilinear Games
2.1 Zero-Sum Games $x^{T}Ay$
Let A be a payoff matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$. We consider the zero-sum game where simultaneously player 1 chooses $x$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, player 2 chooses y in $\mathbb{R}^{p}$, and finally player 2 pays $x^{T}Ay$ to player 1. Here $x$ and y are seen as column vectors. This game has a value which is 0, and both players have optimal strategies:
$\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}x^{T}Ay=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}x^{T}Ay=0.$
Recall that $\mathcal{S}(A)\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}$, and $\mathcal{S}(A)=\{0\}$ if and only if $A=0$. If A and B are matrices of the same size, then $AB^{T}$ and $B^{T}A$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues. The set of optimal strategies of player 1 is $\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},x^{T}A=0\}=Ker(A^{T})$, and the set of optimal strategies of player 2 is $\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p},Ay=0\}=Ker(A)$. So $(x,y)$ is a Nash equilibrium (or saddle-point) of the game if and only if $x\in Ker(A^{T})$ and $y\in Ker(A)$.

Definition 2.1. The Gradient Descent Ascent is defined by:
$\forall t\ge0,\begin{cases}x_{t+1}&=&x_{t}&+&\eta Ay_{t},\\ y_{t+1}&=&y_{t}&-&\eta A^{T}x_{t},\end{cases}$
where $\eta>0$ is a fixed parameter (the gradient step), and $(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$ is the initialization.

Notice that the fixed points of the GDA are exactly the Nash equilibria of the game. It is well known that the GDA does not converge in general, as one can see in the simple "Matching Pennies" case where $n=p=1$ and $A=(1)$, so that $x^{T}Ay$ is simply $xy$. In this case, we obtain:
$\begin{cases}x_{t+1}&=&x_{t}+\eta y_{t}\\ y_{t+1}&=&y_{t}-\eta x_{t}\end{cases}$
which implies $x_{t+1}^{2}+y_{t+1}^{2}=(x_{t}^{2}+y_{t}^{2})(1+\eta^{2})$, and $\|(x_{t},y_{t})\|\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow\infty}+\infty$ as soon as $(x_{0},y_{0})\ne(0,0)$. To overcome this problem, [21] (see also [7]) introduced "optimism" to GDA.

Definition 2.2. The Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent (OGDA) is defined by:
$\forall t\ge0,\begin{cases}x_{t+1}&=&x_{t}&+&2\eta Ay_{t}&-&\eta Ay_{t-1},\\ y_{t+1}&=&y_{t}&-&2\eta A^{T}x_{t}&+&\eta A^{T}x_{t-1}\end{cases}$,
where $\eta>0$ is a fixed parameter, and $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$ is the initialization.

The term of time t-1 is here to thwart the divergence of GDA, by bringing back toward the center the iterate of time $t+1$. We can see empirically the importance of this "optimistic" addition in figure 1 for the matching pennies problem where $A=(1)$. We will see our optimistic GDA as a dynamical system in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$.

Definition 2.3. Define the matrix
$\Lambda=(\begin{matrix}I_{n}&2\eta A&0&-\eta A\\ -2\eta A^{T}&I_{p}&\eta A^{T}&0\\ I_{n}&0&0&0\\ 0&I_{p}&0&0\end{matrix})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+p+n+p)\times(n+p+n+p)},$
and for $t\ge0$, let $Z_{t}$ be the column vector
$Z_{t}=(\begin{matrix}x_{t}\\ y_{t}\\ x_{t-1}\\ y_{t-1}\end{matrix})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}.$
The matrix $\Lambda$ has already been introduced in [19]. We now have: $Z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and $\forall t\ge0$, $Z_{t+1}=\Lambda Z_{t}$ (3).
In the sequel we will denote by I the identity matrix of size $2(n+p)$.

2.2 Convergence of OGDA for a zero-sum game $x^{T}Ay$
The convergence of OGDA when $A=(1)$ is well known and can be shown as follows. We have
$\Lambda=(\begin{matrix}1&2\eta&0&-\eta\\ -2\eta&1&\eta&0\\ 1&0&0&0\\ 0&1&0&0\end{matrix})$
with characteristic polynomial $\det(\Lambda-\lambda I)=\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}+\eta^{2}(1-2\lambda)^{2}$. Assuming $\eta<1/\sqrt{3}$ easy computations show that the spectral radius of $\Lambda$ is strictly smaller than 1. Then $\Lambda^{t}\Rightarrow0$ by Gelfand's theorem, so $(x_{t},y_{t})\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow\infty}(0,0)$.

We now generalize this linear algebra approach to any matrix A, focusing not only on the convergence property but also on the characterization of the limit and on the speed of convergence.

Theorem 2.4. Let $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ with $A\ne0$ and $\eta>0$. We write $\mu_{\max}=\max\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}, \mu\in Sp(AA^{T})\}$; $\mu_{\min}=\min\{\mu>0, \mu\in Sp(AA^{T})\}>0$, and consider the Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent:
$\forall t\ge0,\begin{cases}x_{t+1}&=&x_{t}+2\eta Ay_{t}-\eta Ay_{t-1},\\ y_{t+1}&=&y_{t}-2\eta A^{T}x_{t}+\eta A^{T}x_{t-1}\end{cases}$ (4)
If $\eta>\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$ there exist initial conditions where the corresponding OGDA does not converge. In the sequel we assume that
$\eta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$
Fix an initial condition $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$, and denote by D be the distance from the initialization to the set $Ker(\Lambda-I)$. We have the following:
1) $(x_{t},y_{t})_{t}$ converges to the Nash equilibrium $(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})$, where $x_{\infty}$ is the orthogonal projection of $x_{0}$ onto $Ker(A^{T})$ and $y_{\infty}$ is the orthogonal projection of $y_{0}$ onto Ker(A).
2) If $\eta<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ the convergence is exponential and for all $t\ge0$, $\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C D \lambda_{\max}^{t}$ with $\lambda_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})}$, and $C=\sqrt{\frac{2}{1-\sqrt{\frac{1+5\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}}{2+\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}}}}}$.
3) If $\eta\ge\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ we extend the definition of $\lambda_{\max}$ to $\lambda_{\max}=\max\{\lambda_{*},\lambda_{**}\}$, with
$\lambda_{*}=\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\min}\le\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})}$ and $\lambda_{**}=\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\max}\ge\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{2\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}+\eta\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}\sqrt{4\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}-1}}$.
3a) If $\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}\notin\mathcal{S}(A)$, the convergence is exponential with ratio $\lambda_{\max}$ and for all $t\ge0$, we have $\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C D \lambda_{\max}^{t},$ with $C=\max\{\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\min}<\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{1-\sqrt{\frac{1+5\eta^{2}\mu_{*}}{2+\eta^{2}\mu_{*}}}}},\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\max}>\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{1-\sqrt{\frac{2+\eta^{2}\mu_{**}}{1+5\eta^{2}\mu_{**}}}}}\},$
$\mu_{*}=\max\{\mu\in\mathcal{S}(A),\eta\sqrt{\mu}<1/2\}$ and $\mu_{**}=\min\{\mu\in\mathcal{S}(A),\eta\sqrt{\mu}>1/2\}$.
3b) If $\eta\ge\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ and $\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}\in\mathcal{S}(A)$, then for every $\lambda>\lambda_{\max}$ there exists a constant $C^{\prime}$ such that for all $t\ge0$, $\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C^{\prime}D\lambda^{t}$.

The proof of Theorem 2.4 is in section A.1 of the Appendix. The most delicate aspects are the control of the angles between eigenvectors of the matrix $\Lambda$ for parts 2) and 3a), and the analysis of the Jordan normal form of $\Lambda$ for part 3b).

2.3 Comments on Theorem 2.4
Several remarks are in order.
a) Theorem 2.4 also holds for $A=0$ if we use the convention $\lambda_{\max}=0$ in this case.
b) Both $\lambda_{*}$ and $\lambda_{**}$ are in [0,1) and $\lambda_{\max}\in[\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},1)$. Part 3a) generalizes part 2) of the Theorem, since if $\eta<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ we have $\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\min}<\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}=1$, $\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\max}\ge\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}=0$ and $\mu_{*}=\mu_{\max}$.
c) $Ker(\Lambda-I)$ is the set $\{(\begin{matrix}x\\ y\\ x\\ y\end{matrix}), (x, y) \text{ Nash equilibrium}\}$. Since it contains 0, $D\le\|(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\|=\|Z_{0}\|$.
d) Notice that the speed of convergence is independent of the dimension: $\lambda_{\max}$ as well as C and D from parts 2) and 3a) of the Theorem, only depends on $\mathcal{S}(A)$ and $\eta$, and not on n nor p.
e) The step-size $\eta$ is fixed here, and does not evolve with time. If $\eta < \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ the ratio of convergence $\lambda_{\max}$ is better when $\eta$ is large, so that having $\eta$ too small is not a good option for large t. We elaborate on the optimal choice of $\eta$ in subsection 2.5.
f) When looking at the expression of the limit $(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})$ with regard to the initialization $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})$ one should notice that $x_{\infty}$, somehow surprisingly, only depends on $x_{0}$, and that $y_{\infty}$ only depends on $y_{0}$. Thus, $y_{0}$, $y_{-1}$ and $x_{-1}$ will only have an influence of the curve $(x_{t})_{t}$ but not on its limit.
g) Our ratio $\lambda_{\max}$ is indeed optimal for any matrix $A\ne0$. Indeed, Lemma A.10 in the Appendix shows that there exists a real vector $Z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+p+n+p}$ and a constant $c>0$ such that $\|Z_{t}\|>c\lambda_{\max}^{t}$. This shows that our result improves on the existing literature. We still do a tour below (see g), h), i) and j)) of the main related papers, to see how Theorem 2.4 compare with their results. This tour is summarized in Table 1, where we only mention our results related to the case where $\eta<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$.

h) [5] was the first paper to study last iterate convergence of OGDA in saddle point problems. They consider the case of a matrix A with $\mu_{\max}\le1$ and $0<\eta<\frac{1}{3}\mu_{\min}^{2},$ where $\mu_{\min}>0$ is the smallest positive eigenvalue of $A^{T}A$. Assuming $x_{-1}=x_{0}$ and $y_{-1}=y_{0}$, and introducing $δ_{t}=\|A^{T}x_{t}\|^{2}+\|Ay_{t}\|^{2}$ they show that for $t\ge2$,
$δ_{t}\le(1-\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}^{2})δ_{t-1}+16\eta^{3}δ_{0}$, (5)
This does not prove convergence of $(δ_{t})_{t}$ to 0 but shows that $δ_{t}$ is small for large t and small $\eta$.

i) Let us now compare our result to the more recent work [15] of Liang and Stokes. Theorem 2.4 holds for any initialization $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})$ for any matrix and for any small enough value of $\eta$, not only for a particular value of $\eta$ and squared invertible matrices, as in the paper of Liang and Stokes. Consider for instance the particular case where $\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}=1/8$, studied in [15] for the particular case of A square invertible. We still denote by $\mu_{\min}>0$ the smallest eigenvalue of $A^{T}A$. Liang and Stokes obtained:
$\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(0,0)\|\le4\sqrt{2}\lambda_{L}^{t}\max\{\|(x_{0},y_{0})\|,\|(x_{1},y_{1})\|\},$ with $\lambda_{L}=\exp(-\frac{\mu_{\min}}{16\mu_{\max}})$ (7)
Since $\|(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\|\le\sqrt{2}\max\{\|(x_{0},y_{0})\|,\|(x_{-1},y_{-1})\|\}$, the constants in the above inequality (7) is just slightly bigger than the constant $C_{\eta}$ we find for $\eta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\mu_{\max}}}$. Moreover, by comparing the derivative of the functions $f(x)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-x/2})}$ and $g(x)=\exp(-\frac{x}{16})$, we can show that $\lambda_{\max}=f(\frac{\mu_{\min}}{\mu_{\max}})\le\lambda_{L}=g(\frac{\mu_{\min}}{\mu_{\max}})$ for all $\frac{\mu_{\min}}{\mu_{\max}}\in[0,1]$, showing that in addition to generalizing the convergence results of Liang and Stokes to all matrices, we also improve their bound.

j) Another exponential bound can be seen in [19]. They showed that for any learning rate $\eta$,
$\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C_{P}\lambda_{P}^{t}$
where $\lambda_{P}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-\mu_{\min}\eta^{2}}}$. The value of $\lambda_{P}$ is still higher than $\lambda_{\max}$ for any value of $\eta$ and of A, and their is no control on the constant $C_{P}$.

k) In [17], Mokhtari et al. showed once again the convergence of OGDA for square invertible matrices and for a particular value of $\eta$. For $\eta$ such that $\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}=1/40$, they show:
$\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le\lambda_{M}^{t}\hat{r}_{0}$
where $\lambda_{M}=\sqrt{1-\frac{\mu_{\min}}{6400\mu_{\max}}}$. Once again, it can be shown that for any value of $\mu_{\min}$, $\lambda_{\max}\le\lambda_{M}$.

l) We finally illustrate a part of Theorem 2.4: the expression of the limit value of OGDA with respect to the initialization. Here, we are interested in the independence of the target vector $(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})$ with regard to the vectors $(x_{-1},y_{-1})$. For this, we plotted in Figure 2 several runs of OGDA for the game with matrix $A=(\begin{matrix}1&-1\\ -1&1\end{matrix})$. On the left image, we plotted the first coordinate of $x_{t}$ with the first coordinate of $y_{t},$ and on the right image, we plotted the second coordinate of $x_{t}$ with the second coordinate of $y_{t}$. For each of the run, the same vector $(x_{0},y_{0})$ was chosen, and different values were taken for $x_{-1}$ and $y_{-1}$. We can see that this change of value changes the dynamic of the system mostly at the beginning, but that all of the experiments still converge to the same target point, which only depends on the vector $(x_{0},y_{0})$, as one can see from Theorem 2.4.

2.4 Zero-sum Games $x^{T}Ay+b^{T}x+c^{T}y+d$
Let us now consider the general bilinear case, where we add to the term $x^{T}Ay$, a linear function in $(x, y)$. Fix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$, $c\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and $d\in\mathbb{R}.$ We look at the zero-sum game where simultaneously player 1 chooses $x$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, player 2 chooses y in $\mathbb{R}^{p}$, and finally player 2 pays the quantity $g(x,y)=x^{T}Ay+b^{T}x+c^{T}y+d$ to player 1. The Nash equilibria are the vectors $(x,y)$ such that $g(x,y)=\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(x^{\prime},y)=\inf_{y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}g(x,y^{\prime})$, i.e. such that $A^{T}x+c=0$ and $Ay+b=0.$ Here, there exists a Nash equilibrium if and only if -b is in the range of A and -c is in the range of $A^{T}$. The next result is the extension of Theorem 2.4 to this setup. The proof is in Appendix A.2.

Theorem 2.5. Let $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\backslash\{0\}$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$, $c\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$, $d\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\eta>0$. We write $\mu_{\max} = \max\{\mu\in \mathbb{R}, \mu\in Sp(AA^{T})\}$, $\mu_{\min}=\min\{\mu>0, \mu\in Sp(AA^{T})\}$, and consider the Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent:
$\forall t\ge0, \begin{cases} x_{t+1} &=& x_{t}+2\eta(Ay_{t}+b)-\eta(Ay_{t-1}+b), \\ y_{t+1} &=& y_{t}-2\eta(A^{T}x_{t}+c)+\eta(A^{T}x_{t-1}+c) \end{cases}$ (8)
If the set $\{(x,y)|A^{T}x+c=0,Ay+b=0\}$ of Nash equilibria is empty, then $(x_{t},y_{t})_{t}$ diverges. If $\eta>\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$ there exist initial conditions where the corresponding OGDA does not converge. In the sequel we assume that the set of Nash equilibria is not empty and that $\eta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$. Fix any initial condition $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$, and let D be the distance from $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})$ to the set $\{(x,y,x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}, A^{T}x + c = 0, Ay+b=0\}$. We have the following:
1) $(x_{t},y_{t})_{t}$ converges to the Nash equilibrium $(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})$, where $x_{\infty}$ is the orthogonal projection of $x_{0}$ onto $\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|A^{T}x+c=0\}$ and $y_{\infty}$ is the orthogonal projection of $y_{0}$ onto $\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}|Ay+b=0\}$.
2) If $\eta<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ the convergence is exponential and for all $t\ge0,$ $\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C D \lambda_{\max}^{t}$ with $\lambda_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})},$ and $C=\sqrt{\frac{2}{1-\sqrt{\frac{1+5\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}}{2+\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}}}}}$.
3) If $\eta\ge\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ we extend the definition of $\lambda_{\max}$ to $\lambda_{\max}=\max\{\lambda_{*},\lambda_{**}\}$, with
$\lambda_{*}=\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\min}\le\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})}$ and $\lambda_{**}=\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\max}\ge\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{2\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}+\eta\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}\sqrt{4\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}-1}}.$
3a) If $\eta\ge\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ and $\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}\notin\mathcal{S}(A)$, the convergence is exponential with ratio $\lambda_{\max}$ and for all $t\ge0$, $\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C D \lambda_{\max}^{t}$ with
$C=\max\{\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\min}<\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{1-\sqrt{\frac{1+5\eta^{2}\mu_{*}}{2+\eta^{2}\mu_{*}}}}},\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\max}\ge\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{1-\sqrt{\frac{2+\eta^{2}\mu_{**}}{1+5\eta^{2}\mu_{**}}}}}\},$
$\mu_{*} = \max\{\mu\in \mathcal{S}(A), \eta\sqrt{\mu}<1/2\}$ and $\mu_{**}=\min\{\mu\in\mathcal{S}(A),\eta\sqrt{\mu}>1/2\}.$
3b) If $\eta\ge\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ and $\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}\in\mathcal{S}(A)$, then for every $\lambda>\lambda_{\max}$ there exists a constant $C^{\prime}$ such that for all $t\ge0, \|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C^{\prime}D\lambda^{t}.$

2.5 Optimal geometric ratio of convergence
Given a matrix $A\ne0$ one can ask which $\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}})$ minimizes the geometric ratio of convergence $\lambda_{\max}=$
$\max\{\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\min}\le\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})}, \mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\max}\ge\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{2\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}+\eta\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}\sqrt{4\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}-1}}\}$
Let us introduce the variable $\alpha=\frac{\mu_{\min}}{\mu_{\max}}\in(0,1].$ Computations show that the optimal values for $\eta$ and $\lambda_{\max}$ are:
$\eta^{*}\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{32\alpha}(3+6\alpha-\alpha^{2}-(1-\alpha)\sqrt{(1-\alpha)(9-\alpha)})}\in[\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}],$
$\lambda_{\max}^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{8}(3+6\alpha-\alpha^{2}-(1-\alpha)\sqrt{(1-\alpha)(9-\alpha)})})}.$
For instance, if $A=(\begin{matrix}0&0&0\\ 0&1&0\\ 0&0&2\end{matrix})$, $\mu_{\min}=1$, $\mu_{\max}=4$, $\eta^{*}\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}\simeq0.5608$ and $\lambda_{\max}^{*}\simeq0.956$. When $\alpha$ varies from 0 to 1, $\eta^{*}\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}$ decreases from $\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\simeq0.577$ to $1/2$ and $\lambda_{\max}^{*}$ decreases from 1 to $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. We always have $\eta^{*}\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}\ge1/2$, justifying our interest for the cases where $\eta\ge\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ in Theorems 2.4 and 2.5. Convergence is faster when $\alpha$ increases, and is optimal when $\alpha=1$, i.e. when $AA^{T}$ has a unique nonzero eigenvalue. It is for instance the case when A is (up to a multiplicative constant) an orthogonal matrix.

3 General-Sum Bilinear Games
We extend the analysis of section 2 to the class of general-sum bilinear games. For simplicity we will only consider here values of $\eta$ corresponding to case 2) of Theorems 2.4 and 2.5.

3.1 General-Sum Games $(x^{T}Ay,x^{T}By)$
We consider here the case of a non zero-sum game given by two matrices A and B in $\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$. Simultaneously player 1 chooses $x$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and player 2 chooses y in $\mathbb{R}^{p}$, then the payoff to player 1 is $x^{T}Ay$ and the payoff to player 2 is $x^{T}By$. By definition, $(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$ is a Nash equilibrium of the game if:
$x^{T}Ay=\max_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}x^{\prime T}Ay$ and $x^{T}By=\max_{y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}x^{T}By^{\prime}.$
The set of Nash equilibria is $Ker(B^{T})\times Ker(A)$, which always contains (0,0). In section 2 we have considered the case where $B=-A$. We now introduce the OGDA for general-sum games:

Definition 3.1. The Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent algorithm for the general-sum game $(x^{T}Ay,x^{T}By)$ is defined by:
$\forall t \ge 0, \begin{cases} x_{t+1} &=& x_{t} + 2\eta Ay_{t} - \eta Ay_{t-1}, \\ y_{t+1} &=& y_{t} + 2\eta B^{T}x_{t} - \eta B^{T}x_{t-1} \end{cases}$ (9)
where $\eta>0$ is a fixed parameter, and $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$ is the initialization.

Notice that if $(x_{t},y_{t})_{t}$ converges, the limit is a Nash equilibrium. We first observe that there exist general-sum games $(x^{T}Ay,x^{T}By)$ for which OGDA does not converge. A simple case is when $A=B=(1)$. Assume for simplicity that $x_{0}=y_{0}$ and $x_{-1}=y_{-1}$. Then for all t, $x_{t}=y_{t}$ and $x_{t+1}=x_{t}(1+2\eta)-\eta x_{t-1}$. This can be written $x_{t+1}-x_{t}=\eta x_{t}+\eta(x_{t}-x_{t-1})$, so that if $x_{0}>\max\{x_{-1},0\}$ then $x_{t+1}>\max\{x_{t},0\}$ for all t. It implies $x_{t+1}>x_{t}(1+\eta)$ for all t, so $x_{t}\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow\infty}+\infty$. We have obtained:

Proposition 3.2. Convergence of OGDA may fail for general-sum games $(x^{T}Ay,x^{T}By)$. In other words, for any $\eta>0$, there exist some initialization $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})$ such that $(x_{t},y_{t})_{t}$ does not converge.

We now present a simple trick, a modification of OGDA which ensures convergence to Nash equilibria in general-sum games when $\eta$ is small enough. The set of Nash equilibria of the game $(x^{T}Ay,x^{T}By)$ is $Ker(B^{T})\times Ker(A)$. The set $Ker(B^{T})$ is the set of optimal strategies of player 1 in the zero-sum game $x^{T}(-B)y$ (where player 1 wants to minimize the payoff $x^{T}By)$ and $Ker(A)$ is the set of optimal strategies of player 2 in the zero-sum game $x^{T}Ay$ (where player 2 wants to minimize $x^{T}Ay$). As a consequence, the convergence of OGDA for zero-sum games gives here a simple algorithm converging to a Nash equilibrium of a general-sum game.

Definition 3.3. The Double Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent (DOGDA) for the general-sum game $(x^{T}Ay,x^{T}By)$ is defined by:
$\begin{cases} x_{t+1} &=& x_{t} - 2\eta By_{t}^{\prime} + \eta By_{t-1}^{\prime}, \\ y_{t+1}^{\prime} &=& y_{t}^{\prime} + 2\eta B^{T}x_{t} - \eta B^{T}x_{t-1}, \\ x_{t+1}^{\prime} &=& x_{t}^{\prime} + 2\eta Ay_{t} - \eta Ay_{t-1}, \\ y_{t+1} &=& y_{t} - 2\eta A^{T}x_{t}^{\prime} + \eta A^{T}x_{t-1}^{\prime} \end{cases}$ (10)
where $\eta>0$ is a fixed parameter, and $z_{0}:=(x_{0},y_{0}^{\prime},x_{0}^{\prime},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1}^{\prime},x_{-1}^{\prime},y_{-1})\in(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p})^{4}$ is the initialization.

Proposition 3.4. Convergence of DOGDA to a Nash Equilibrium. Let A, $B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ and $0<\eta<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$, where $\mu_{\max}=\max\{\rho(A^{T}A),\rho(B^{T}B)\}$. Consider $z_{0}\in(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p})^{4}$ and the sequence $(x_{t},y_{t}^{\prime},x_{t}^{\prime},y_{t})$ induced by the DOGDA algorithm (10) with initialization $z_{0}$. Then $(x_{t},y_{t})$ converges to a Nash equilibrium $(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})$, $x_{\infty}$ is the orthogonal projection of $x_{0}$ on $Ker(B^{T})$ and $y_{\infty}$ is the orthogonal projection of $y_{0}$ on $Ker(A)$. Moreover the convergence is exponential: for all $t\ge0,$ $\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C \lambda_{\max}^{t}\|z_{0}\|$, where $C=\sqrt{2}(1-\sqrt{\frac{1+5\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}}{2+\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}}})^{-1/2}$ and $\lambda_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}^{\prime}})}$, with $\mu_{\min}^{\prime}=\min\{\mu>0,\mu\in\mathcal{S}(A)\cup\mathcal{S}(B)\}$ and the convention $\lambda_{\max}=0$ if $A=B=0$.
Proof. Directly comes from Theorem 2.4, since DOGDA is the juxtaposition of 2 uncoupled OGDA dynamics. $\square$

Remark 3.5. One can replace B with $B$ and/or A with $A$ in the OGDA or DOGDA algorithms. This is a strong specificity of our bilinear games. We have chosen (10) in definition 3.3 because of the following remark 3.6.

Remark 3.6. The DOGDA trick is due to the following property of our bilinear games: if $g_{1}$ denotes the payoff function $x^{T}Ay$ for player 1 and $g_{2}$ is the payoff function $x^{T}By$ for player 2, the Nash equilibria of the game $(g_{1},g_{2})$ are the couples (x, y) where $x$ is optimal for player 1 in the zero-sum game $(-g_{2},g_{2})$ and y is optimal for player 2 in the zero-sum game $(g_{1},-g_{1})$.

This property will also hold for the general-sum bilinear games of section 3.3, so the DOGDA trick also applies there. One can ask how often this property holds in general? In the case of general-sum games with 2 players and 2 actions for each player played with mixed strategies, assuming that the 8 real coefficients are a priori independently selected according to the uniform law on a given compact interval with positive length, one can show that the probability to obtain a game where this property holds is $2/9\simeq22\%$.

3.2 Sufficient conditions for convergence in games $(x^{T}Ay,x^{T}By)$
We come back to the analysis of the OGDA algorithm (9) and partly extend the analysis of section 2 to the non zero-sum case. Define here the matrix:
$\Lambda_{A,B}=(\begin{matrix}I_{n}&2\eta A&0&-\eta A\\ 2\eta B^{T}&I_{p}&-\eta B^{T}&0\\ I_{n}&0&0&0\\ 0&I_{p}&0&0\end{matrix})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+p+n+p)\times(n+p+n+p)}.$
For $t\ge0$ let again $Z_{t}$ be the column vector $Z_{t}=(\begin{matrix}x_{t}\\ y_{t}\\ x_{t-1}\\ y_{t-1}\end{matrix})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}.$ We have $\forall t\ge0, Z_{t+1}=\Lambda_{A,B}Z_{t}.$ Define now for each $\mu\in\mathbb{C}$,
$S^{*}(\mu)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}=\mu\eta^{2}(1-2\lambda)^{2}\}$,
and recall the notation $\mathcal{S}(A,B)=Sp(B^{T}A)\cup Sp(AB^{T})$. One can show (see Appendix A.3)

Proposition 3.7. $Sp(\Lambda_{A,B})=\bigcup_{\mu\in\mathcal{S}(A,B)}S^{*}(\mu).$

"Unfortunately", one can show that for each $\mu\notin\mathbb{R}_{-}$ there exists $\eta_{0}>0$ such that for each $0<\eta<\eta_{0}$ there exists $\lambda$ in $S^{*}(\mu)$ with $|\lambda|>1$. So if there exists $\mu$ in $\mathcal{S}(A,B)\backslash\mathbb{R}_{-}$ there is no hope to prove that for $\eta>0$ small enough the OGDA will converge for any initialization. This is why we will assume $\mathcal{S}(A,B)\subset\mathbb{R}_{-}$ in the following Theorem 3.8, which gives sufficient conditions for convergence of OGDA in general-sum games. It includes the case where $B=-lA$ for some $l>0$ but also, as we will see later, the case where $B=-(A^{\dagger})^{T},$ where $A^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}$ is the generalized Moore-Penrose inverse of A.

Theorem 3.8. Let A, B be in $\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ with $\mathcal{S}(A,B)\subset\mathbb{R}_{-}$. Let $\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}})$, where $\mu_{\max}=\rho(B^{T}A)=\rho(AB^{T})$ is the largest eigenvalue of $B^{T}A.$ Assume that (A and B are square invertible matrices), or that $\Lambda_{A,B}$ is diagonalizable. Given an initialization $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p},$ consider the Optimistic Gradient Descent algorithm (9). Then:
1) $(x_{t},y_{t})_{t}$ converges to a Nash equilibrium $(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})$.
2) $x_{\infty}$ is the linear projection of $x_{0}$ onto $Ker(B^{T})$ along $(Ker(A^{T}))^{\perp}=Im(A)$ and $y_{\infty}$ is the linear projection of $y_{0}$ onto $Ker(A)$ along $(Ker(B))^{\perp}=Im(B^{T})$.
3) The convergence is exponential: there exists a constant $C>0$ verifying for all $t\ge0$,
$\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C\|(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\|\lambda_{\max}^{t}$
where $\lambda_{\max} = \max\{|\lambda|, \lambda\in Sp(\Lambda_{A,B}), \lambda \ne 1\} < 1$, $\lambda_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})},$ with $\mu_{\min} = \min\{\mu>0, -\mu\in \mathcal{S}(A,B)\}$ and the convention $\lambda_{\max}=0$ if $A=B=0$.

Examples 3.9.
1) Consider the case where $A=(\begin{matrix}1&0\\ 2&0\end{matrix})$ and $B=(\begin{matrix}-2&-1\\ 0&0\end{matrix})$. Then $B^{T}A=(\begin{matrix}-2&0\\ -1&0\end{matrix})$ and $AB^{T}=(\begin{matrix}-2&0\\ -4&0\end{matrix})$ so that $\mathcal{S}(A,B)=\{0,-2\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{-}$ and $\mu_{\max}=\mu_{\min}=2$. For $\eta=0.1<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}$, one can check that $\Lambda_{A,B}$ is diagonalizable. Theorem 3.8 applies, so that for any initialization the sequence $(x_{t})$, resp. $(y_{t})$, converges to the projection of $x_{0}$ resp. $y_{0}$, onto the vertical axis $\{(0,z),z\in\mathbb{R}\}$ along $\{(z,2z),z\in\mathbb{R}\}$, resp. $\{(2z,z),z\in\mathbb{R}\}$. The ratio of convergence is $\lambda_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{0.92})}\simeq0.9897.$
2) Consider the case where $A=(\begin{matrix}1&1\\ 1&1\end{matrix})$ and $B=(\begin{matrix}1&1\\ -1&-1\end{matrix})$. Then $B^{T}A=(\begin{matrix}0&0\\ 0&0\end{matrix})$, $AB^{T}=(\begin{matrix}2&-2\\ 2&-2\end{matrix})$ and $\mathcal{S}(A,B)=\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{-}$. However one can show that $\Lambda_{A,B}$ is not diagonalizable, and $(\Lambda_{A,B}^{t})_{t}$ does not converge, so that for many initial conditions OGDA does not converge here.

3.3 Extension to the general bilinear case
We consider here the general case of a non zero-sum game given by matrices A, B in $\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ vectors $b, e$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, $c, f$ in $\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and constants d, g in $\mathbb{R}$. Simultaneously player 1 chooses $x$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and player 2 chooses y in $\mathbb{R}^{p}$, then the payoff for player 1 is
$g_{1}(x,y)=x^{T}Ay+b^{T}x+c^{T}y+d$,
and the payoff for player 2 is
$g_{2}(x,y)=x^{T}By+e^{T}x+f^{T}y+g$.
In section 2.4 we have considered the case where $g_{2}=-g_{1}$. By definition, $(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$ is a Nash equilibrium of the game if:
$g_{1}(x,y)=\max_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}g_{1}(x^{\prime},y)$ and $g_{2}(x,y)=\max_{y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}g_{2}(x,y^{\prime}).$
Here, the set of Nash equilibria is $\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}, B^{T}x+f=0,Ay+b=0\}$, and there exists a Nash equilibrium if and only if -b is in the range of A and -f is in the range of $B^{T}$.

Definition 3.10. The Optimistic Gradient Descent Ascent algorithm for the general-sum game $(x^{T}Ay+b^{T}x+c^{T}y+d,x^{T}By+e^{T}x+f^{T}y+g)$ is given by:
$\forall t\ge0,\begin{cases}x_{t+1}&=&x_{t}&+&2\eta(Ay_{t}+b)&-&\eta(Ay_{t-1}+b),\\ y_{t+1}&=&y_{t}&+&2\eta(B^{T}x_{t}+f)&-&\eta(B^{T}x_{t-1}+f)\end{cases}$ (11)
where $\eta>0$ is a fixed parameter, and $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$ is the initialization.

The fixed points of the OGDA are the Nash equilibria. Notice that the constants d and g play no role here, so we set without loss of generality $d=g=0$. We generalize now Theorem 3.8.

Theorem 3.11. Let A, B be in $\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$, $b, e$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $c, f$ in $\mathbb{R}^{p}$, with $\mathcal{S}(A,B)\subset\mathbb{R}_{-}$. Let $\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}})$, where $\mu_{\max}=\rho(B^{T}A)=\rho(AB^{T})$ is the largest eigenvalue of $B^{T}A.$ Assume that (A and B are square invertible matrices) or that $\Lambda_{A,B}$ is diagonalizable, and assume that a Nash equilibrium exists, i.e. that $\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p},B^{T}x+f=0,Ay+b=0\}\ne\emptyset$. Given an initialization $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$, consider the Optimistic Gradient Descent algorithm (11). Then:
1) $(x_{t},y_{t})_{t}$ converges to a Nash equilibrium $(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})$.
2) $x_{\infty}$ is the linear projection of $x_{0}$ onto $\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},B^{T}x+f=0\}$ along $(Ker(A^{T}))^{\perp}=Im(A)$ and $y_{\infty}$ is the linear projection of $y_{0}$ onto $\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p},Ay+b=0\}$ along $(Ker(B))^{\perp}=Im(B^{T})$.
3) The convergence is exponential: there exists a constant $C>0$ verifying for all $t\ge0$,
$\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C\|(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\|\lambda_{\max}^{t}$
where $\lambda_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})},$ $\mu_{\min}=\min\{\mu>0,-\mu\in\mathcal{S}(A,B)\}$ and the convention $\lambda_{\max}=0$ if $A=B=0$.

3.4 Improving the zero-sum rate of convergence with general-sum games
Consider a zero-sum game $g(x,y)=x^{T}Ay+b^{T}x+c^{T}y+d$ with $A\ne0$, and assume we are interested in finding an optimal strategy y for player 2. We write as before $\mu_{\max}=\max\{\mu>0,\mu\in Sp(A^{T}A)\}$ and $\mu_{\min}=\min\{\mu>0, \mu\in Sp(A^{T}A)\}$.

3.4.1 Using $B=-lA$ for fixed $\eta$
Assume $\eta$ is sufficiently small, in particular smaller than $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$. Theorem 2.5 provides the convergence of OGDA to equilibria of the zero-sum game, with a geometric rate of convergence of:
$\lambda_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})}$,
Consider now applying OGDA to the general sum game $(g(x,y),-lg(x,y))$ for some parameter $l>0$. The matrix $\Lambda_{A,-lA}$ is diagonalizable (as in the zero-sum case, see Lemma A.6 in the appendix), and Theorem 3.11 applies. As in the zero-sum case, $(y_{t})$ converges to the linear projection of $y_{0}$ to $\{y,Ay+b=0\}$ along $(Ker(B))^{\perp}$. The geometric rate is now:
$\lambda_{\max}(l)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4l\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})}$,
which is smaller than $\lambda_{\max}$ for $l\ge1$. So increasing l given $\eta$ fixed can improve the convergence rate. This is valid as long as $4l\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}<1$, and can approach the rate of $\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha})}$. Keeping $\eta$ fixed and increasing l so that $4l\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}\simeq1$, can be seen as an alternative to keeping $l=1$ (zero-sum game) and increasing $\eta$ so that $4l\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}<1$. This may be interesting for instance if several matrix games are considered at the same time and one wants the parameter $\eta$ to be the same in each case.

3.4.2 Using $B=-(A^{\dagger})^{T}$ to improve the convergence rate
Consider the case of $g(x,y)=x^{T}Ay$, with $A=(\begin{matrix}1&0\\ 0&2\end{matrix})$. Then $\mathcal{S}(A)=\{1,4\}$, $\mu_{\max}=4$ and $\mu_{\min}=1$. By Theorem 2.4, applying OGDA with $B=-A$ gives a convergence rate, when $12\eta^{2}<1$ of $\lambda_{\max}=$
$\max\{\mathbf{1}_{4\eta^{2}\le1}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}})}, \mathbf{1}_{16\eta^{2}\ge1}\sqrt{8\eta^{2}+2\sqrt{2}\eta\sqrt{16\eta^{2}-1}}\}$
which for optimal $\eta = \sqrt{\frac{71-3\sqrt{105}}{128}} \simeq 0.2804$, gives the optimal rate $\lambda_{\max}^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{8}(3+6\alpha-\alpha^{2}-(1-\alpha)\sqrt{(1-\alpha)(9-\alpha)})})}$ for $\alpha=1/4$ i.e. $\lambda_{\max}^{*}\simeq0.956$. Define now $B=(\begin{matrix}-1&0\\ 0&-\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix})$. We can run OGDA on the general-sum game $(x^{T}Ay,x^{T}By)$ and Theorem 3.8 applies. Since $B^{T}A=-Id$ we obtain the convergence rate of $\lambda_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}})$, but now under the condition $4\eta^{2}<1.$ This implies that if $\eta$ is chosen optimally close to $1/2$, we get the best possible convergence rate $\lambda_{\max}^{**}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}\simeq0.707.$

Let us come back to the general setup of Theorem 3.11. If $\mathcal{S}(A,B)=\{0\}$, the only case where the theorem applies is the (non interesting) case when $A=B=0$. So let us assume here that $\mathcal{S}(A,B)\ne\{0\}$. Define:
$\mu_{\min}=\min\{\mu>0,\mu\in Sp(-AB^{T})\}$ and $\mu_{\max}=\max\{\mu>0,\mu\in Sp(-AB^{T})\}$.
Theorem 3.11 implies that if $4\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}<1$, then the convergence rate is $\lambda_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})}$. So for $\eta$ optimally chosen, the convergence rate is close to:
$\lambda_{\max}^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{\mu_{\min}}{\mu_{\max}}})}.$
This rate is minimized whenever $\mu_{\min}=\mu_{\max}$, i.e. when $AB^{T}$ and $B^{T}A$ have a unique negative eigenvalue. This is in particular the case if $B^{T}=-A^{-1}$. If A is not square or not invertible, we can use more generally $B^{T}=-A^{\dagger}$ where $A^{\dagger}$ is the Moore-Penrose inverse of A.

Lemma 3.12. Let $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ and $B=-(A^{\dagger})^{T}.$ Then $Sp(AB^{T})\subset\mathbb{R}_{-}$ and for $0<\eta<\frac{1}{2}$ the matrix $\Lambda_{A,B}$ is diagonalizable.

For instance if $A=(\begin{matrix}1&0\\ 0&2\\ 0&0\end{matrix})$ we put $B=(\begin{matrix}-1&0\\ 0&-\frac{1}{2}\\ 0&0\end{matrix})$. We can now apply Theorem 3.8 and obtain:

Theorem 3.13. Let $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $c\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and $0<\eta\le\frac{1}{2}$. Consider the Optimistic Gradient Descent algorithm (11) for the payoffs $g_{1}(x,y)=x^{T}Ay+b^{T}x+c^{T}y$ and $g_{2}(x,y)=x^{T}By+e^{T}x+f^{T}y$ with $B=-(A^{\dagger})^{T}$ and some $e\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$, $f\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$. Then $(y_{t})_{t}$ converges to the orthogonal projection of $y_{0}$ onto $\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p},Ay+b=0\}$, and $(x_{t})_{t}$ converges to the orthogonal projection of $x_{0}$ onto $\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},(A^{\dagger})^{T}x=f\}$. Moreover, the convergence is exponential: if $\eta<1/2$ there exists a constant $C>0$ s.t. for all $t\ge0$, $\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C\|(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\|\lambda_{\max}^{t}$ where $\lambda_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}})}.$ And if $\eta=1/2$ then for every $\lambda>1/\sqrt{2}$ there exists $C>0$ s.t. for all $t\ge0$, $\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C\|(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\|\lambda^{t}$.

The proof follows from Theorem 3.11 and Lemma 3.12, using the general properties $Ker(A^{\dagger})=Ker(A^{T})=Im(A)^{\perp}$ and $Ker(A)=Im(A^{\dagger})^{\perp}$ (see the Appendix for the last sentence of Theorem 3.13). Here also, $\lambda_{\max}$ is the best possible rate for exponential convergence, and if $\eta\simeq\frac{1}{2}$ we obtain the OGDA optimal rate of $\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}\simeq0.707$. Notice that we obtain exactly the same limit for $(y_{t})_{t}$ as in Theorem 2.5 when $B=-A,$ but the speed of convergence is much better here when we use the OGDA algorithm in a non zero-sum context with $B=-(A^{\dagger})^{T}$. Notice also that if $f=A^{\dagger}x^{*}$ for some $x^{*}$ satisfying $A^{T}x^{*}+c=0$ then $(x_{t})_{t}$ also converges to the same limit as in Theorem 2.5, i.e. to the orthogonal projection of $x_{0}$ onto $\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},A^{T}x+c=0\}$. In particular if $c=0$, one can choose $f=0$ to ensure this property.

3.5 "Cooperation" induced by OGDA
Assume the game $(x^{T}Ay,x^{T}By)$ has a "potential for cooperation", in the specific sense that there exist actions $(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$ such that both $x^{T}Ay$ and $x^{T}By$ are positive. Define the sequence $(x_{t},y_{t})_{t}$ by $(x_{t},y_{t})=t(x,y)$ for each t, then both $x_{t}^{T}Ay_{t}\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow+\infty}+\infty$ and $x_{t}^{T}By_{t}\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow+\infty}+\infty$. Even though we are not reaching a Nash equilibrium, this may be seen as a desirable outcome of the interaction between the players, who aim at maximizing their payoffs. A particular case is common-payoff games, for which $A=B$. This includes the case where $A=B=(1)$, for which there is no hope to obtain convergence of OGDA to a Nash equilibrium of the game except if the vectors at initialization are already null. However, we can reach an infinite payoff with OGDA, as was seen in section 3.1. We now generalize this property to a larger group of matrices.

We use the usual version of OGDA for general-sum games, that is algorithm (9), and the matrix describing the dynamics is $\Lambda_{A,B}$ as introduced in section 3.1. In the proof of Theorem 2.4, we showed that a matrix $\Lambda_{A,-A}$ is always diagonalizable. In the general case of matrices A, B, assuming that the spectrum of $B^{T}A$ is included in $\mathbb{R}_{-}$ is not sufficient to conclude that $\Lambda_{A,B}$ is diagonalizable for small $\eta$. However if $A=\alpha B$ for some non-zero parameter $\alpha$, we will show that $\Lambda_{A,B}$ is diagonalizable for $\eta$ small enough. We know from Proposition 3.7 that: $Sp(\Lambda_{A,B})=\bigcup_{\mu\in\mathcal{S}(A,B)}S^{*}(\mu)$ with $S^{*}(\mu)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}=\mu\eta^{2}(1-2\lambda)^{2}\}$. Define $E_{\lambda}$ as the set of complex eigenvectors of $\Lambda_{A,B}$ associated to an eigenvalue $\lambda$.

Lemma 3.14. Assume that $\eta<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$, and that ($A=\alpha B$ for some real $\alpha\ne0$), or (A and B are square matrices in $\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ such that $B^{T}A$ is diagonalizable with n distinct nonzero real eigenvalues). Then $\mathbb{C}^{(n+p+n+p)}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda_{A,B})}E_{\lambda}.$
The proof is in the Appendix.

We now present our last theorem, related to the convergence of OGDA to a Nash equilibrium or to infinite payoffs in the case of general non zero-sum games with payoffs $(x^{T}Ay + b^{T}x + c^{T}y + d, x^{T}By + e^{T}x + f^{T}y + g)$.

Theorem 3.15. Let A, B be in $\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ $b, e$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, $c, f$ in $\mathbb{R}^{p}$, and $d, g$ in $\mathbb{R}$, with $\mathcal{S}(A,B)\subset\mathbb{R}$. Let $\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}})$ where $\mu_{\max}=\rho(B^{T}A)=\rho(AB^{T})$ is the largest eigenvalue of $B^{T}A.$ Assume that $\Lambda_{A,B}$ is diagonalizable in $\mathbb{C}$, and that a Nash equilibrium exists, i.e. that $\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}, B^{T}x+f=0,Ay+b=0\}\ne\emptyset$. Given an initialization $(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}$, consider the Optimistic Gradient Descent algorithm (11). Then either $(x_{t},y_{t})_{t}$ converges exponentially fast to a Nash equilibrium, that is, an element of $\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}, B^{T}x+f=0,Ay+b=0\},$ or the current payoffs $x_{t}^{T}Ay_{t}+b^{T}x_{t}+c^{T}y_{t}+d$ and $x_{t}^{T}By_{t}+e^{T}x_{t}+f^{T}y_{t}+g$ converge exponentially fast to $+\infty$. The assumptions $\mathcal{S}(A,B)\subset\mathbb{R}$ and $\Lambda_{A,B}$ diagonalizable are in particular true in each of the following cases:
1) $B=\alpha A$ for some real number $\alpha\ne0$,
2) A and B are square matrices in $\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ such that $B^{T}A$ is diagonalizable with n distinct nonzero real eigenvalues.
Notice that case 1) includes the case of common payoffs games $A=B$, as well as the case of zero-sum games $B=-A$.

4 Illustration: Generative Adversarial Networks
Algorithms to approximate probability distributions and generate new data from some unknown laws are more and more needed. Despite this necessity, the theory behind this problem is lacking, resolving in inconsistent performances. The main type of generative algorithms are Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) introduced in [11] by Goodfellow et al.

4.1 GANs and WGANS
In GANs, two neural networks (a discriminator and a generator) are set in competition against each other. The goal of the generator is to generate new data as close as possible to the true data. And in the original version of GANs, the discriminator aims at recognizing true data from the created ones. We will here mostly consider Wasserstein GANs, a very popular sort of GANs that was introduced by Arjovsky in [3] and uses the Wasserstein distance between probability distributions $\nu$ and $\nu^{\prime}$, defined by
$W_{1}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})=\sup_{f\in Lip_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{\prime}}[f].$
There is a true distribution $\nu^{*}$, and we (the generator) want to generate a distribution as close as possible to $\nu^{*}$. Unfortunately, we do not have access to the true distribution, but only to an empirical approximation $\hat{\nu}$, via some samples generated according to $\nu^{*}$. We also cannot choose any distribution but are restricted to choose a parameter $\theta$, and we want to find the parameter minimizing the Wasserstein distance between the generated distribution and the empirical distribution $\hat{\nu}$. More precisely, the generator $G_{\theta}$ is a usual neural network from a latent space $\mathbb{R}^{m}$ to the feature space $\mathbb{R}^{d}$. From a Gaussian distribution $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m})$ it defines a probability measure $\nu_{\theta}$, which is the law of the random variable $G_{\theta}(Z)$. In order to find the generated measure which is the closest to $\hat{\nu}$ we want to find the parameter $\theta$ minimizing the Wasserstein distance between $\hat{\nu}$ and $\nu_{\theta}$. This is given by the problem
$\inf_{\theta} W_{1}(\hat{\nu}, \nu_{\theta}) = \inf_{\theta} \sup_{f \in Lip_{1}} \mathbb{E}_{\hat{\nu}}[f] - \mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\theta}}[f]$
where f plays the role of the discriminator. However, in practice, we cannot compute the supremum over 1-Lipschitz function, thus we need to approximate this set using neural networks. We parameterize the set of discriminators, and denote by $D_{\beta}$ the 1-Lipschitz neural network from $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ to $\mathbb{R}$ with parameter $\beta$. In practice, we want the set $\{D_{\beta}|\beta\}$ to be dense in the set of 1-Lipschitz function (as shown possible, for instance, in Anil et al. in [2]). The WGAN problem we finally want to solve can thus be written as
$\inf_{\theta}\sup_{\beta}\mathcal{L}(\theta,\beta)$ where $\mathcal{L}(\theta,\beta)=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\nu}}[D_{\beta}]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\theta}}[D_{\beta}]$ (12)

4.2 First example
We now look at a simple illustrative example introduced by Daskalakis et al. in [5], and see how our theoretical bound of convergence looks like. We assume that the data follows a multivariate normal distribution of mean $v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{n}$: $x \sim \mathcal{N}(v, I_{d})$, where $v$ is unknown, and we make the simplifying assumption $\hat{\nu}=\nu^{*}.$ We begin from a multivariate normal distribution Z with mean 0: $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})$, and study linear generators of the form $G_{\theta}(z)=z+\theta$. For the set of discriminators, we use the very small set of all $D_{\beta}: x\mapsto\langle\beta,x\rangle$, where $\|\beta\|_{2}\le1$ so that $D_{\beta}$ is 1-Lipschitz. The WGAN problem becomes:
$\inf_{\theta}\sup_{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(v,I_{n})}[\langle\beta,x\rangle]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})}[\langle\beta,z+\theta\rangle]$,
that is
$\inf_{\theta}\sup_{\beta}\langle\beta,v-\theta\rangle$.
We are in the case $x^{T}Ay+b^{T}x+c^{T}y+d$ of section 2.4 with $A=-I_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ (hence $\mu_{\max}=1)$, $b=v, c=0$ and $d=0. \theta$ is arbitrary in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. A priori we have the constraint $\|\beta\|_{2}\le1$ but allowing $\|\beta\|$ arbitrary does not change the value nor the optimal strategies of the generator, so we simply skip the constraint and consider any $\beta$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. Theorem 2.5 implies here that for $\eta<\frac{1}{2}$, OGDA will converge exponentially fast, with ratio $\lambda_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}})}$. Running OGDA on this example with $\eta=0.3$ gives us $\lambda_{\max}=\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\approx0.949$ and $C\approx2.197$. We plotted the graph of the distance of OGDA to the set of Nash equilibria with regard to time, on figure 3, where we took $n=2$ and $v=(\begin{matrix}3\\ 4\end{matrix})$. In the pictures, we see the exponential convergence of OGDA for different values of $\eta$. We also plotted the different bounds that were found previously. In the first picture, $\eta$ is close to the maximum possible level, so the convergence is really fast. We have seen in section 2.3 that our ratio $\lambda_{\max}$ is the best possible, and these figures mark well this fact: the plots of the log of the distance to the limit Nash equilibrium and of the log of the bound we found are parallel. However, if $\eta=0.03$ our constant C is not optimal, as can be seen by the space between the two parallel lines.

For most of the different bounds found in the literature, the higher $\eta$ is, the fastest the convergence occurs. This result can be seen in figure 3, as the bound given by the different papers, as well as the true speed of convergence of OGDA is faster for $\eta=0.3$ compared to $\eta=0.03$. The previous papers gave results only for $\eta\le\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$ and we know that, for this range of $\eta$, whatever the matrix A, the theoretical bounds on OGDA will be the fastest when $\eta$ equals $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$. What was shown here in section 2 is that OGDA will still converge for any $\eta\in[\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}})$ and that for all matrices, the optimal $\eta$ is in this range.

In addition to this amelioration, a final step toward a better convergence rate has been done in subsection 3.4.2, where transforming the zero-sum game into a general-sum game using $B=-(A^{\dagger})^{T}$ converging to the same limit point with higher speed greatly boost the convergence to a Nash equilibrium of the non-zero game. This allows us to have the best possible exponential ratio: $\lambda_{\max}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ which improves a lot all previous theoretical rates. In the present case where $A=-I_{n}$ we already have $-A=-(A^{\dagger})^{T}$ so there is no gain in changing the game.

4.3 Generalization
We generalize here the previous example, still keeping the linear structure. We assume that the data follows a multivariate normal distribution of mean $v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$: $\nu^{*}=\mathcal{N}(v,I_{d}),$ where v is unknown, and we make the simplifying assumption $\hat{\nu}=\nu^{*}.$ Here $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m})$ with $m\ll d$ and study linear generators of the form $G_{\theta}(z)=\theta+A_{1}z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},$ for some matrix $A_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}.$ For the set of discriminators, we use the set of all $D_{\beta}:x\mapsto\langle A_{2}\beta,x\rangle$, for $\|\beta\|_{2}\le1$, with $A_{2}$ a fixed matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfying $\rho(A_{2}^{T}A_{2})\le1$ so that $D_{\beta}$ is 1-Lipschitz for each $\beta$. The WGAN problem becomes:
$\inf_{\theta}\sup_{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(v,I_{d})}[\langle A_{2}\beta,x\rangle]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m})}[\langle A_{2}\beta,\theta+A_{1}z\rangle]$,
that is
$\inf_{\theta}\sup_{\beta}\langle A_{2}\beta,v-\theta\rangle$.
We are in the case $x^{T}Ay+b^{T}x+c^{T}y+d$ of section 2.4 with $x=\beta, y=\theta, A=-A_{2}^{T}, b=A_{2}^{T}v, c=0$ and $d=0. \theta$ is arbitrary in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and a priori we have the constraint $\|\beta\|_{2}\le1$ but again we skip the constraint which does not change the value nor the optimal strategy of the generator. The set of Nash equilibria is then $Ker(A_{2}^{T})\times\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n},v-\theta\in Ker(A_{2})\}$.

We now consider the final amelioration given in subsection 3.4.2. Assume $A_{2}^{T}\ne0$ and define $\mu_{\max}=\max\{\mu,\mu\in Sp(A_{2}^{T}A_{2})\}\le1, \mu_{\min}=\min\{\mu>0,\mu\in Sp(A_{2}^{T}A_{2})\}$ and $\alpha=\frac{\mu_{\min}}{\mu_{\max}}$. Running OGDA with $\eta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$, Theorem 2.5 ensures that $(\beta_{t})$ converges to the orthogonal projection of $\beta_{0}$ onto $Ker(A_{2}^{T})$, and $(\theta_{t})$ converges to the orthogonal projection of $\theta_{0}$ on $\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n},v-\theta\in Ker(A_{2})\}$. The geometric ratio of convergence $\lambda_{\max}$ is $\max\{\lambda_{*},\lambda_{**}\}$, where
$\lambda_{*}=\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\min}\le\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})}$ and $\lambda_{**}=\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\max}\ge\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{2\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}+\eta\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}\sqrt{4\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}-1}}$
And for an optimal $\eta$, we obtain the optimal rate of
$\lambda_{\max}^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{8}(3+6\alpha-\alpha^{2}-(1-\alpha)\sqrt{(1-\alpha)(9-\alpha)})})}\in[\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},1).$
We can introduce $B=-(A^{\dagger})^{T} = A_{2}^{\dagger}$ and run OGDA for the general-sum game $(g_{1}(\beta, \theta), g_{2}(\beta, \theta))$ where $g_{1}(\beta, \theta) = \beta^{T}(-A_{2}^{T})\theta + \beta^{T}A_{2}^{T}v$ and $g_{2}(\beta, \theta) = \beta^{T}B\theta$. According to Theorem 3.11, OGDA now converges to the same limit but with geometric ratio $\lambda_{\max}^{\prime}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}})}$ which, for $\eta=1/2$, is $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ , the best possible ratio. And this is true for all matrices $A_{2}$ and vector v.

Example: $-A_{2}^{T}=A=(\begin{matrix}1&0\\ 0&1/2\end{matrix})$ and $v=(1,1)$. In the zero-sum game, P1 maximizes $g_{1}(\beta,\theta)=\beta^{T}A\theta+\beta^{T}Av,$ whereas P2 minimizes $g_{1}(\beta,\theta)$. Running OGDA for the zero-sum game, for $\eta<\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}\simeq0.577$ induces the convergence of $(\beta_{t},\theta_{t})$ to (0, v), with optimal geometric ratio of convergence $\lambda_{\max}^{*}\simeq0.9538$ for $\eta^{*}\simeq0.5608$. Let us run OGDA on the general-sum game where player 1 still maximizes $g_{1}(\beta,\theta)$ and player 2 now maximizes $g_{2}(\beta,\theta)=\beta^{T}B\theta$, with $B=-(A^{\dagger})^{T} = (\begin{matrix}-1&0\\ 0&-2\end{matrix})$. By Theorem 3.13, $(\beta_{t},\theta_{t})$ still converges to the same limit (0, v), and if $\eta\simeq1/2$ the convergence ratio is now the best possible convergence rate $\lambda_{\max}^{**}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\simeq0.707$. The speed of convergence can be seen on the figure 4. Using a general-sum game with the matrix $A^{\dagger}$ greatly improves the speed of convergence of OGDA.

4.4 Discussion and limitations
Our results concern bilinear games, which can be seen as toy-models compared to the complexity of real WGANs. To go toward more realism, several issues could be considered. In practice, neural networks have millions of parameters, if not billions, and they suffer from the curse of dimensionality. However, OGDA will not suffer more than GDA in this account, because OGDA, as GDA is a first order method with a single oracle call per iteration. The only difference is that OGDA needs to store previous gradient, and thus needs more memory space than GDA. For both of these algorithms, the speed of convergence to an equilibria is independent of the dimensions n and p in the case of bilinear games. Another issue is to take into account the stochastic character of the answer that we receive. In practice, we won't exactly have access to $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(v,I_{d})}[\langle\alpha,x\rangle]$, nor to $\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}[\langle\alpha,z+\theta\rangle]$, but only to empirical averages of data following the according distribution. In [5], Daskalakis et al. already considered this problem. A deeper study on stochasticity for OGDA and Extra-Gradient was done by Hsieh et al. in [13]. They showed a geometric convergence of the expected distance to the saddle points for strongly concave/convex games, with a bound depending on the variance of the noise. A further step is not to assume the unboundedness of the problem. In this article like in many others, we assume that the set of parameters is an Euclidean space: we are doing minimization and maximization on $\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and $\mathbb{R}^{n}$. But in reality, neural networks are computer-based, thus the set of possible neural-networks is far more complex. However, the goal is to model the largest possible set of functions, and the maxima and minima reached by the weights are very large, and will get larger if float maximum bit-size gets higher. 5 Conclusion
We have proved the exponential convergence of OGDA to a Nash equilibrium for any bilinear game, and also provided the best ratio for the convergence. This implies an important stability property for the OGDA system as the number of steps increases. In case of multiple Nash equilibria, we characterized the limit equilibrium as a function of the initialization, and gave a simple expression of it. We also improved the range of convergence of OGDA with regard to the gradient step $\eta$ in the case of zero-sum games. These results clarify and generalize all previous results of the literature on the topic. We also extended the study to general-sum games. We presented a simple trick to guarantee, via a variant of OGDA, convergence to Nash equilibria in our general-sum games. We also showed that in an important class of games (including the common payoffs case as well as the zero-sum case) either OGDA converges to a Nash equilibrium, or the payoffs of both players converge to $+\infty$. We gave sufficient conditions for OGDA to converge to a Nash equilibrium of a general-sum game, and showed how to increase the speed of convergence of a min-max problem involving a payoff matrix A by introducing a general-sum game using the matrix $-(A^{\dagger})^{T}$, i.e. minus the transpose Moore-Penrose inverse of A. This may be seen as a proof of concept that non-zero sum games can be used to solve efficiently Minmax or zero-sum problems. Extending this approach to general, non necessarily bilinear, games, seems promising and is left for future research. The bilinear games we have studied are widely used as a toy model for many applications, and we illustrated our results on GAN examples. These may be used as an additional argument in favor of using the Optimistic version of GDA in GANs. Nevertheless, if one wants to go further in the application to GANs, there are several factors that should be taken into account. One of them would be the stochasticity of the payoffs. A.1 Proof of Theorem 2.4
We fix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\ne0$ and $\eta>0$.
A.1.1 Convergence to Nash equilibria for general $\eta>0$
Recalling the characteristic polynomial of $\Lambda$ in the particular case where $A=(1),$ we introduce the following sets:
Definition A.1. Given $\mu\in\mathbb{C},$ we define
$S(\mu)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}+\mu\eta^{2}(1-2\lambda)^{2}=0\}$.
Notice that if $\eta=0$ or $\mu=0$ then $S(\mu)=\{0,1\}$. Simple computations imply the following lemma.
Lemma A.2. Assume $\mu$ is real non-negative. We write $X=\eta\sqrt{\mu}\ge0,$ and let $δ\in\mathbb{C}$ be such that $δ^{2}=1-4X^{2}.$ More precisely, if $4X^{2}\le1$ we use $δ=\sqrt{1-4X^{2}}$ and if $4X^{2}\ge1$ we use $δ=i\sqrt{4X^{2}-1}$.
Then we can write $S(\mu)=\{\lambda_{1}(\mu),\lambda_{2}(\mu),\lambda_{3}(\mu),\lambda_{4}(\mu)\},$ with $\lambda_{1}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}(1+δ+2iX)=\overline{\lambda_{3}}(\mu)$ and $\lambda_{2}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}(1-δ+2iX)=\overline{\lambda_{4}}(\mu).$
0) If $X=0$, then $S(\mu)=\{0,1\}$.
1) If $0<X<1/2$, $S(\mu)$ has 4 elements and $|\lambda_{1}(\mu)|^{2}=|\lambda_{3}(\mu)|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4X^{2}})<1,$ and $|\lambda_{2}(\mu)|^{2}=|\lambda_{4}(\mu)|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(1-\sqrt{1-4X^{2}})<\frac{1}{2}.$
2) If $X=1/2$ $S(\mu)=\{\frac{1}{2}(1+i),\frac{1}{2}(1-i)\}$.
3) If $X>1/2$, $S(\mu)$ has 4 elements and $|\lambda_{1}(\mu)|^{2}=|\lambda_{3}(\mu)|^{2}=2X^{2}+X\sqrt{4X^{2}-1}$ and $|\lambda_{2}(\mu)|^{2}=|\lambda_{4}(\mu)|^{2}=2X^{2}-X\sqrt{4X^{2}-1}$.
Notice that for $X\ge1/2$, the property $2X^{2}+X\sqrt{4X^{2}-1}<1$ is equivalent to $X<\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$.
The link between the eigenvalues of the matrices $A^{T}A$ and $AA^{T},$ and the eigenvalues of $\Lambda$ is the following.
Lemma A.3. $Sp(\Lambda)=\bigcup_{\mu\in\mathcal{S}(A)}S(\mu)$
Proof. For $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ and $Z=(x^{T},y^{T},x^{\prime T},y^{\prime T})^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}$, we have:
$\Lambda Z=\lambda Z\iff\begin{cases}x+2\eta Ay-\eta Ay^{\prime}=\lambda x\\ y-2\eta A^{T}x+\eta A^{T}x^{\prime}=\lambda y\\ x=\lambda x^{\prime}\\ y=\lambda y^{\prime}\end{cases}\iff\begin{cases}\lambda(1-\lambda)x^{\prime}+(2\lambda-1)\eta Ay^{\prime}=0\\ \lambda(1-\lambda)y^{\prime}-(2\lambda-1)\eta A^{T}x^{\prime}=0\\ x=\lambda x^{\prime}\\ y=\lambda y^{\prime}\end{cases}$
a) Assume $\Lambda Z=\lambda Z$ and $Z\ne0$. Multiplying the first line by $(2\lambda-1)\eta A^{T}$ and the second line by $(2\lambda-1)\eta A$, we get:
$\begin{cases}\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}y^{\prime}+(2\lambda-1)^{2}\eta^{2}A^{T}Ay^{\prime}=0\\ \lambda^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}x^{\prime}+(2\lambda-1)^{2}\eta^{2}AA^{T}x^{\prime}=0\end{cases}$ (13)
Since $Z\ne0$ we have $x^{\prime}\ne0$ or $y^{\prime}\ne0$ and $\lambda\ne1/2.$ This implies that $-\frac{\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}}{(2\lambda-1)^{2}\eta^{2}}$ is an eigenvalue of $A^{T}A$ or of $AA^{T}$. If we denote by $\mu$ such eigenvalue, then $\lambda\in S(\mu)$.
b) Conversely, let $\mu$ be an eigenvalue of $AA^{T}$ and consider $\lambda\in S(\mu)$. Let $x^{\prime}\ne0$ be such that $AA^{T}x^{\prime}=\mu x^{\prime}$ and consider $y^{\prime}$ satisfying:
$\lambda(1-\lambda)y^{\prime}=\eta(2\lambda-1)A^{T}x^{\prime}$
If $\mu\ne0$ then $\lambda\notin\{0,1\}$ and $y^{\prime}$ is uniquely defined; if $\mu=0$ then $AA^{T}x^{\prime}=0$ so $A^{T}x^{\prime}=0$ and any $y^{\prime}$ will do. In each case, one can check that $\Lambda(\lambda x^{\prime},\lambda y^{\prime},x^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{T}=\lambda Z$, so that $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $\Lambda$. This also stands for an eigenvalue of $A^{T}A$. a) and b) show that the set of eigenvalues of $\Lambda$ is $\{\lambda|\lambda\in S(\mu), \mu\in\mathcal{S}(A)\}$. $\square$
Combining Lemma A.2 and Lemma A.3 yields;
Corollary A.4. $\max\{|\lambda|,\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)\backslash\{1\}\}<1\iff\forall\mu\in\mathcal{S}(A),\eta\sqrt{\mu}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\iff\eta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$
And if $\eta>\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$ we have $\max\{|\lambda|, \lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)\} > 1$, and for some initial conditions the OGDA diverges. In the sequel of the proof of Theorem 2.4, we always assume that $\eta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$.
Consider $\mu$ in $\mathcal{S}(A)$. Since $A^{T}A$ and $AA^{T}$ are positive semi-definite, $\mu$ is a non-negative real number. If $\mu\ne0, S(\mu)\subset\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C},|\lambda|<1\}$ by Lemma A.2. If $\mu=0$ then $S(\mu)=\{0,1\}$. By Lemma A.3, we have: $Sp(\Lambda)\subset\{1\}\cup\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C},|\lambda|<1\}.$
Let us define $\lambda_{\max}=\max\{|\lambda|,\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)\backslash\{1\}\}<1.$ One can check that $\lambda_{\max}=\max\{\lambda_{*},\lambda_{**}\}$ with $\lambda_{*}=\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\min}\le\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}})}$ and $\lambda_{**}=\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{\max}\ge\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}\sqrt{2\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}+\eta\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}\sqrt{4\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}-1}}$. Notice that if $\eta\le\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}$, $\lambda_{\max}=\lambda_{*}$.
We now describe the eigenspaces of $\Lambda$. For each $\lambda$ in $\mathbb{C}$, we write $E_{\lambda}=\{Z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p},\Lambda Z=\lambda Z\}$. The proof of Lemma A.3 easily gives:
Lemma A.5. Let $\lambda$ be in $Sp(\Lambda)$, and $\mu\ge0$ in $\mathcal{S}(A)$ uniquely defined by $\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}+\mu\eta^{2}(1-2\lambda)^{2}=0$.
If $\lambda=0$ then $\mu=0\in\mathcal{S}(A)$ and $E_{0}=\{(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}, x = 0, y=0, Ay^{\prime} = 0, A^{T}x^{\prime}=0\}$, so $\dim(E_{0})=\dim(Ker(A))+\dim(Ker(A^{T}))$.
If $\lambda=1$, then $\mu=0\in\mathcal{S}(A)$ and $E_{1}=\{(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p},x=x^{\prime}, y=y^{\prime}, Ay^{\prime}=0, A^{T}x^{\prime}=0\}$, so $\dim(E_{1})=\dim(Ker(A))+\dim(Ker(A^{T}))$.
If $\lambda\notin\{0,1\}$, then $\mu>0$ and $E_{\lambda}=\{(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}, x=\lambda x^{\prime}, y=\lambda y^{\prime}, AA^{T}x^{\prime}=\mu x^{\prime}, y^{\prime} = \frac{(1-\lambda)\lambda}{\eta(2\lambda-1)\mu}A^{T}x^{\prime}\}$, so $\dim(E_{\lambda})=\dim(Ker(AA^{T}-\mu I))$.
We can now sum the dimension of the eigenspaces.
Lemma A.6. $\sum_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)}\dim(E_{\lambda})=2(n+p)-2\dim(Ker(A^{T}A-\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}I)).$
Proof. Recall that $rank(A^{T}A)=rank(A)=rank(A^{T})$ and, $A^{T}A$ being positive semi-definite, $rank(A^{T}A)=\sum_{\mu>0}\dim(Ker(A^{T}A-\mu I)).$ Recall also from Lemma A.2 that $S(\mu)$ has 2 elements if $\mu=\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}$.
$\sum_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)}\dim(E_{\lambda})=\dim(E_{0})+\dim(E_{1})+\sum_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda),\lambda\notin\{0,1\}}\dim(E_{\lambda})$
$=2\dim(Ker(A))+2\dim(Ker(A^{T}))+2\dim(Ker(A^{T}A-\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}I))+\sum_{\mu\in Sp(A^{T}A),\mu\ne0,\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}}4\dim(Ker(A^{T}A-\mu I))$
$=2\dim(Ker(A))+2\dim(Ker(A^{T}))+4 rank(A^{T}A)-2\dim(Ker(A^{T}A-\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}I))$
$=2(rank(A)+\dim(Ker(A)))+2(rank(A^{T})+\dim(Ker(A^{T})))-2\dim(Ker(A^{T}A-\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}I))$
$=2(n+p)-2\dim(Ker(A^{T}A-\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}I)).$ $\square$
We can already notice that $\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}\notin\mathcal{S}(A)$ is equivalent to $\sum_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)}\dim(E_{\lambda})=2(n+p)$, i.e. is equivalent to the property that $\Lambda$ is diagonalizable in $\mathbb{C}$. The next lemma will be used to characterize the limit of the OGDA.
Lemma A.7. If y is an eigenvector of $A^{T}A$ associated to a nonzero eigenvalue, then y is orthogonal to $Ker(A)$. Similarly if $x$ is an eigenvector of $AA^{T}$ associated to a nonzero eigenvalue, then $x$ is orthogonal to $Ker(A^{T})$.
Proof. Let $\hat{y}$ be a vector in the kernel of A, and let $\mu\ne0$ be the eigenvalue of $A^{T}A$ associated with y. Then: $\mu\langle y,\hat{y}\rangle=\langle\mu y,\hat{y}\rangle=\langle A^{T}Ay,\hat{y}\rangle=\langle Ay,A\hat{y}\rangle=\langle Ay,0\rangle=0$. Hence, $\langle y,\hat{y}\rangle=0$ for any $\hat{y}$ in $Ker(A)$. The proof for eigenvectors of $AA^{T}$ is similar.

A.1.2 Convergence to Nash equilibria if $\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}\notin\mathcal{S}(A)$
We assume here that $\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}\notin\mathcal{S}(A)$, with $0<\eta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$. Here $Ker(A^{T}A-\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}I)$ is empty, so $\sum_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)}\dim(E_{\lambda})=2(n+p)$ and $\Lambda$ is diagonalizable. Since $\max\{|\lambda|,\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)\backslash\{1\}\}<1$ the matrix $\Lambda^{t}$ converges as $t\rightarrow\infty,$ and the convergence is exponential with ratio $\lambda_{\max}=\max\{|\lambda|,\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)\backslash\{1\}\}$. Consider an initial condition $Z_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})$. $Z_{0}$ can be uniquely written: $Z_{0}=\sum_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)}z_{\lambda}$, with $z_{\lambda}\in E_{\lambda}$ for all $\lambda$. For all $t\ge0$, $Z_{t}=(x_{t},y_{t},x_{t-1},y_{t-1})=\Lambda^{t}Z_{0}=\sum_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)}\lambda^{t}z_{\lambda}$ and $Z_{t}\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow\infty} Z_{\infty}:=z_{1}$. We have obtained the convergence of $Z_{t}$ to the projection of $Z_{0}$ onto $E_{1}=Ker(\Lambda-I)$ along $\bigoplus_{\lambda\ne1}Ker(\Lambda-\lambda I)$. Since $Z_{\infty}\in E_{1}$, $(x_{t})_{t}$ converges to a limit $x_{\infty}$ in $Ker(A^{T})$ and $(y_{t})_{t}$ converges to a limit $y_{\infty}$ in $Ker(A)$. Moreover there exists a constant $C^{\prime},$ only depending on A and $\eta$ through the matrix $\Lambda$, such that for all $t\ge0 \|\Lambda^{t}-\Lambda^{\infty}\|\le C^{\prime}\lambda_{\max}^{t}$. And for all $t\ge0$, $\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C^{\prime}\lambda_{\max}^{t}$.
Now, let us write $z_{\lambda}$ as $(\hat{x}_{\lambda},\hat{y}_{\lambda},\hat{x}_{\lambda}^{\prime},\hat{y}_{\lambda}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}$ for each $\lambda$ in $Sp(\Lambda)$. We have $y_{0}=\sum_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)}\hat{y}_{\lambda}$, and $(y_{t})_{t}$ converges to $y_{\infty}=\hat{y}_{1}\in Ker(A)$. $\hat{y}_{0}=0$ and $\sum_{\lambda\notin\{0,1\}}\hat{y}_{\lambda}$ is orthogonal to $Ker(A)$ thanks to Lemma A.7, so we obtain that $\hat{y}_{1}=y_{\infty}$ is the orthogonal projection of $y_{0}$ onto $Ker(A)$. In the same way, one can prove that $x_{\infty}$ is the orthogonal projection of $x_{0}$ onto $Ker(A^{T})$.

A.1.3 Convergence to Nash equilibria if $\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}\in\mathcal{S}(A)$
We assume here that $\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}\in\mathcal{S}(A)$, with $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}\le\eta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$. Consider $\mu=\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}>0.$ By assumption, $\mu$ is an eigenvalue of $AA^{T}$ and $A^{T}A$, and $S(\mu)=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\}$ with $\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(1+i)$ and $\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(1-i)$. We know that $\dim(Ker(\Lambda-\lambda_{1}I))=\dim(Ker(\Lambda-\lambda_{2}I))=\dim(Ker(AA^{T}-\mu I))$, and $\sum_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)}\dim(E_{\lambda})=2(n+p)-2\dim(Ker(A^{T}A-\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}I))<2(n+p).$ so $\Lambda$ is not diagonalizable in $\mathbb{C}$. If $0\notin\mathcal{S}(A)$, then by Gelfand's theorem the OGDA converges to 0, and $Ker(A)=\{0\}$. To fix ideas we now assume here that $0\in\mathcal{S}(A)$. The eigenvalues of $\Lambda$ are 1 and eigenvalues with modulus $<1$.

Lemma A.8. The algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda=1$ in the characteristic polynomial of $\Lambda$ is $\dim E_{1}$.
Proof of Lemma A.8 Let $Z=(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}$ be such that $(\Lambda-I)^{2}Z=0$. Then $(\Lambda-I)Z\in E_{1}$ i.e. $2\eta Ay-\eta Ay^{\prime}=x-x^{\prime}, -2\eta A^{T}x+\eta A^{T}x^{\prime}=y-y^{\prime}, A^{T}(x-x^{\prime})=0$ and $A(y-y^{\prime})=0$. Then $Ay=Ay^{\prime}, A^{T}x=A^{T}x^{\prime}, \eta Ay^{\prime}=x-x^{\prime}$ and $-\eta A^{T}x^{\prime}=y-y^{\prime}$. It implies $A^{T}Ay^{\prime}=0$, which is equivalent to $Ay^{\prime}=0$. Similarly $AA^{T}x^{\prime}=0$, so $A^{T}x^{\prime}=0$ and finally $Z\in E_{1}$. We have shown that $Ker(\Lambda-I)^{2}=Ker(\Lambda-I)$ so for each $l\ge2$, $Ker(\Lambda-I)^{l}=Ker(\Lambda-I)$, and the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda=1$ is $\dim E_{1}$. $\square$

Looking at the Jordan normal form of $\Lambda$, we can conclude that $\Lambda^{t}$ converges to a projection matrix $\Lambda^{\infty}$. For any initial condition $Z_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0},x_{-1},y_{-1})$; $Z_{t}$ converges to an element of $E_{1}$, so $(x_{t})_{t}$ converges to a limit $x_{\infty}$ in $Ker(A^{T})$ and $(y_{t})_{t}$ converges to a limit $y_{\infty}$ in $Ker(A)$. Moreover for every $\lambda>\lambda_{\max}$ there exists a constant $C^{\prime}$ such that for all $t>0$, $\|\Lambda^{t}-\Lambda^{\infty}\|\le C^{\prime}\lambda^{t}$. This implies $\|(x_{t},y_{t})-(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\|\le C^{\prime}\lambda^{t}$ for all t. To conclude, it remains to show that $x_{\infty}$, resp. $y_{\infty}$ is the orthogonal projection of $x_{0}$, resp. $y_{0},$ onto $Ker(A^{T})$, resp. $Ker(A)$.

Lemma A.9. $\dim(Ker(\Lambda-\lambda_{1}I)^{2})=\dim(Ker(\Lambda-\lambda_{2}I)^{2})=2\dim(Ker(AA^{T}-\mu I)).$ This also holds if $0\notin\mathcal{S}(A)$. Moreover, if $(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in(Ker(\Lambda-\lambda_{1}I))^{2}$, then $x$ and $x^{\prime}$ are in $(Ker(A^{T}))^{\perp}$ and y and $y^{\prime}$ are in $(Ker(A))^{\perp}$.
Proof of Lemma A.9 We write the proof for $\lambda_{1}$ only. Consider $Z=(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{p}.$ Computations show that $Z\in(Ker(\Lambda-\lambda_{1}I))^{2}$ if and only if:
$\begin{cases}2\eta A(2y-y^{\prime})&=&i(2x-x^{\prime})\\ -2\eta A^{T}(2x-x^{\prime})&=&i(2y-y^{\prime})\\ \eta A(y-\lambda_{1}y^{\prime})&=&\frac{i}{2}(x-\lambda_{1}x^{\prime})\\ \eta A^{T}(x-\lambda_{1}x^{\prime})&=&-\frac{i}{2}(y-\lambda_{1}y^{\prime}).\end{cases}$
This implies $AA^{T}(2x-x^{\prime})=\mu(2x-x^{\prime})$ and $AA^{T}(x-\lambda_{1}x^{\prime})=\mu(x-\lambda_{1}x^{\prime}).$ Conversely, choose independently any 2 vectors $x_{1}^{*}$ and $x_{2}^{*}$ in $Ker(AA^{T}-\mu I)$ and define uniquely x, $x^{\prime}$ y and $y^{\prime}$ by:
$\begin{cases}2x-x^{\prime}&=&x_{1}^{*}\\ x-\lambda_{1}x^{\prime}&=&x_{2}^{*}\\ i(2y-y^{\prime})&=&-2\eta A^{T}(x_{1}^{*})\\ -\frac{i}{2}(y-\lambda_{1}y^{\prime})&=&\eta A^{T}(x_{2}^{*}).\end{cases}$
Then one can check that $(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})$ belongs to $Ker(\Lambda-\lambda_{1}I)^{2}$. $Ker(AA^{T}-\mu I)\subset Ker(A^{T})^{\perp}$ by Lemma A.7, so $x_{1}^{*}$ and $x_{2}^{*}$ belong to $Ker(A^{T})^{\perp}$. So $x$ and $x^{\prime}$ also belong to the vector subspace $Ker(A^{T})^{\perp}$, and similarly y and $y^{\prime}$ belong to $Ker(A)^{\perp}$. The map $(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*})\mapsto(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})$ is linear one-to-one onto $Ker(AA^{T}-\mu I)\times Ker(AA^{T}-\mu I)$, so $\dim(Ker(\Lambda-\lambda_{1}I)^{2})=2\dim(Ker(AA^{T}-\mu I))$. $\square$

Looking at the dimensions, we obtain that the algebraic dimension of both eigenvalues $\lambda_{1}$ and $\lambda_{2}$ of $\Lambda$ is $2\dim(Ker(AA^{T}-\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}I))$, and for $i=1,2,$ the characteristic subspace of $\lambda_{i}$ is $(Ker(\Lambda-\lambda_{i}I))^{2}$ for $i=1,2$. Using the spectral decomposition of $\Lambda$, the initial vector $Z_{0}$ can now be uniquely written: $Z_{0}=\sum_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)}z_{\lambda},$ with $z_{\lambda}\in E_{\lambda}$ for $\lambda\notin\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\}$ and $z_{\lambda_{i}}\in Ker(\Lambda-\lambda_{i}I)^{2}$ for $i=1,2$. $Z_{t}=\Lambda^{t}Z_{0}\Rightarrow Z_{\infty}:=z_{1}$ so $(x_{t})_{t}$ converges to the first component $\tilde{x}_{1}\in Ker(A^{T})$ of $z_{1}$. By lemmas A.7 and A.9, $x_{0}-\tilde{x}_{1}$ belong to $(Ker(A^{T}))^{\perp}$ so $\tilde{x}_{1}$ is the orthogonal projection of $x_{0}$ onto $Ker(A^{T})$. Similarly, $(y_{t})_{t}$ converges to the orthogonal projection of $y_{0}$ onto $Ker(A)$. This concludes the proofs of parts 1) and 3) of Theorem 2.4.

Lemma A.10. Let $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ with $A\ne0$, and $0<\eta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$. Then there exist $Z_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+p+n+p}$ and a constant $c>0$ such that $\|Z_{t}^{\prime}\|>c\lambda_{\max}^{t}$.
Proof. If $\lambda_{\max}=\lambda_{*},$ consider $\lambda=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1-4\eta^{2}\mu_{\min}}+2i\eta\sqrt{\mu_{\min}})$, and lemmas A.2 and A.3 will imply that $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $\Lambda$ with modulus $\lambda_{\max}<1$. Similarly, if $\mu_{\max}\ge\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}$ and $\lambda_{\max}=\lambda_{**}$ consider $\lambda=\frac{1}{2}(1+i\sqrt{4\eta^{2}\mu_{\max}-1}+2i\eta\sqrt{\mu_{\max}})$, and similarly lemmas A.2 and A.3 will imply that $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $\Lambda$ with modulus $\lambda_{\max}<1$. In both cases, consider $Z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+p+n+p}\ne0$ such that $\Lambda Z_{0}=\lambda Z_{0}$, we have for all t, $Z_{t}=\lambda^{t}Z_{0}\Rightarrow0$. So for all $t\ge0$, $\|(x_{t},y_{t},x_{t-1},y_{t-1})-0\|=\lambda_{\max}^{t}\|Z_{0}\|$. Now, the initialization $Z_{0}^{\prime}$ that we should consider should have all of its coefficient real, while $Z_{0}$ may not be in $\mathbb{R}^{n+p+n+p}.$ Let us prove the existence of a real vector $Z_{0}^{\prime}$ verifying the lemma. For $\lambda$ an eigenvalue of $\Lambda$, let us denote by $F_{\lambda}$ the kernel $Ker(\Lambda-\lambda I)^{\nu(\lambda)}$ where $\nu(\lambda)$ is the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$. Then, $\mathbb{C}^{n+p+n+p}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)}F_{\lambda}$. Remark that if $\Lambda$ is diagonalizable, $F_{\lambda}=E_{\lambda}$ for all $\lambda$. Because $Vect_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{n+p+n+p})=\mathbb{C}^{n+p+n+p},$ there exist a vector $Z_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+p+n+p}$ such that its projection on $F_{\lambda_{\max}}$ along $\bigoplus_{\lambda\in Sp(\Lambda)\backslash\{\lambda_{\max}\}}F_{\lambda}$ is nonzero. Let us call $z_{\lambda_{\max}}$ this projection. Then, because $\lambda_{\max}$ is the biggest eigenvalue strictly smaller than 1, there exits a constant $c>0$ with $\|\Lambda^{t}(Z_{0}^{\prime}-Z_{\infty}^{\prime})\|\sim\|\Lambda^{t}z_{\lambda_{\max}}\|>c\lambda_{\max}^{t}$ proving the wanted result.

A.1.4 Speed of convergence when $0<\eta<\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\mu_{\max}}}$ and $\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}}\notin\mathcal{S}(A)$
We prove here part 2) of Theorem 2.4.
